---
title: "Attention is a design material"
description: "Interfaces do not merely display information. They borrow a scarce resource and either return it intact or spend it carelessly."
pubDate: 2026-10-03
---

We talk about typography, color, and layout as design materials. Attention belongs on that list too. Every screen asks for some of it. Every animation spends a little. Every notification tries to renew the lease. Products that ignore this end up looking busy even when they are technically correct.

Attention is scarce in a way pixels are not. You can add another panel. You cannot mint another hour in someone else’s day. That asymmetry should make designers and builders cautious. Instead, many interfaces behave like they are competing in a local tournament for gaze, even when the long-term goal is trust.

A calm product treats attention as something to borrow carefully and return promptly. It puts the next useful action close at hand. It avoids decorative motion that explains nothing. It refuses to dress status as urgency. The result is not boredom. The result is a feeling that the software respects the user’s time enough to get out of the way.

There is a difference between emphasis and interruption. Emphasis helps a person notice what matters inside a task they already chose. Interruption recruits them into a task they did not choose. Both can be justified. Only one should be common. When interruption becomes the default language of a product, users learn to armor themselves. They mute, skim, and distrust. That armor is rational, and it is expensive to redesign around later.

I have come to distrust dashboards that greet you with eight competing numbers. They look like command centers, which flatters the maker, but they often fail a simpler job: helping a person decide what to do next. A single honest number with a clear verb is frequently more valuable than a mosaic of charts. Density is not the same as usefulness.

Writing is part of this. Labels, empty states, and error messages are attention machinery. Vague copy forces people to interpret. Precise copy lets them move. The best microcopy feels almost invisible because it matches the user’s internal model closely enough that reading barely feels like work.

There is also a moral dimension that product culture sometimes shrugs off. If your growth loop depends on reclaiming attention people were trying to spend elsewhere, you should at least be able to say so without euphemism. “Engagement” can mean helping someone finish a meaningful task. It can also mean training a reflex. Those are different businesses wearing the same vocabulary.

Designing with attention in mind changes the review questions. Instead of asking only whether a feature is discoverable, ask whether discovering it costs more than it returns. Instead of asking whether an animation is delightful, ask whether it teaches. Instead of asking whether a badge creates urgency, ask whether the urgency is real.

None of this requires a sparse aesthetic as dogma. A dense tool can still be attentive if its density is earned by the work. Pilots need dense cockpits. Casual readers do not. The material question is fit: how much attention does this moment deserve, and are we spending more than that for our own convenience?

If attention is a design material, then wasting it is a kind of defect. It may not crash the build. It still leaves a mark on the relationship between person and product. The products I trust most are the ones that seem slightly embarrassed to ask for my focus — and determined to make the asking worthwhile.
