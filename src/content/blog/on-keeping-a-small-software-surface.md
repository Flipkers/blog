---
title: "On keeping a small software surface"
description: "Most products accumulate surface area until nobody can see the whole thing. Small surfaces are a discipline, not an accident."
pubDate: 2026-09-12
---

Most software starts small by necessity. There is one screen, one workflow, one promise. Then the product succeeds enough to invite requests, and the requests invite features, and the features invite settings, and the settings invite documentation that nobody reads. What began as a tool becomes a landscape.

A large surface is not always wrong. Spreadsheets are large because the world they model is large. But many products become large for a quieter reason: it is easier to add than to decide. Adding feels like progress. Deciding feels like conflict. Over time the product becomes a museum of unresolved opinions.

Keeping a small surface is less about minimalism as an aesthetic and more about preserving a single sentence of meaning. If a stranger asks what the product does, can you answer without a comma splice? If the answer requires three “also” clauses, the surface has already started to sprawl.

Small surfaces are kinder to users. They reduce the number of places a person can get lost, and they make competence arrive sooner. They are also kinder to the people who maintain the product. Every extra control is a permanent tax on testing, support, and redesign. The tax compounds quietly until a rewrite starts to look romantic.

The hard part is that users will ask for more, and many of those asks will be reasonable in isolation. The discipline is to ask a second question: does this belong to the product’s center, or is it a satellite that only looks important because someone is standing next to it today? Satellites can live elsewhere — in exports, integrations, or a separate tool — without polluting the core.

There is a useful test I return to. Imagine removing a feature and rewriting the homepage. If the homepage becomes clearer, the feature was probably diluting the product. If the homepage becomes dishonest, the feature was load-bearing. Most contested features fail the first version of this test and pass only after someone invents a sentence that flatters them.

Another test is emotional. Large surfaces often create a low-grade anxiety in the people who own them. There is always one more edge case, one more toggle, one more onboarding tip. Small surfaces create a different feeling: a slight fear that you are under-serving someone, paired with the relief of knowing what you are for. That trade is usually worth making.

Keeping software small does not mean freezing it. It means growing by deepening rather than widening — making the existing path faster, clearer, and more trustworthy. Depth compounds. Width scatters. Users feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

If you are starting something new, protect the first sentence. Write it down. Put it where the roadmap can see it. When a request arrives, check whether it sharpens that sentence or softens it. Softening is how products lose their outline. And once the outline is gone, no amount of polish will make the software feel inevitable again.
